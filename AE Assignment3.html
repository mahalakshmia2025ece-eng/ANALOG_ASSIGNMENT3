What is an Oscillator?

An oscillator is an electronic circuit that generates a continuous periodic signal, such as a sine wave or square wave, without requiring a periodic input signal.

It uses a DC power supply, an amplifying element and a feedback network to sustain oscillations.

Barkhausen criterion (ideal steady-state condition):
Loop gain magnitude |Aβ| = 1
Total loop phase shift = 0° or an integer multiple of 360°.

Types of Oscillators

1. RC Oscillators

Use resistors and capacitors to set the frequency. Commonly used at audio frequencies.

Types: RC Phase Shift, Wien Bridge.

2. LC Oscillators

Use inductors and capacitors as a resonant network. Commonly used for radio-frequency signals.

Types: Hartley, Colpitts.

3. Crystal Oscillator

Uses a piezoelectric crystal, usually quartz, to obtain highly stable oscillation frequency.

Key feature: High frequency stability.

RC Oscillators: Compare the Two Types

RC Phase Shift Oscillator

A transistor or amplifier works with a feedback network containing three RC sections. The RC network provides phase shift; the amplifier supplies the remaining phase shift and gain.

For three identical RC sections:
f ≈ 1 / (2πRC√6)

This formula assumes the standard idealized three-section network with equal R and C values.

Wien Bridge Oscillator

Uses a lead-lag RC bridge network. It is widely used to generate low-distortion sine waves.

For equal R and C:
f = 1 / (2πRC)

The gain and stabilization network must also be designed for sustained oscillation.

LC Oscillators: Compare the Two Types

Hartley Oscillator

Uses a capacitive element and an inductive network with a tapped inductor or two inductors. Feedback is obtained from the inductive divider.

f ≈ 1 / (2π√(LeqC))

For coupled inductors, the equivalent inductance depends on the connection and mutual coupling.

Memory trick: Hartley → L for inductors.

Colpitts Oscillator

Uses an inductor and two capacitors forming a capacitive divider. Feedback is taken from the capacitor network.

Ceq = (C1 × C2) / (C1 + C2)
f ≈ 1 / (2π√(L Ceq))

Memory trick: Colpitts → capacitors are split.

Crystal Oscillator

A crystal oscillator uses the mechanical resonance of a piezoelectric crystal to control the oscillation frequency. It provides excellent frequency stability compared with many ordinary RC and LC oscillators.

  • High frequency stability.
  • Used for clock and timing references.
  • Frequency depends on the crystal and circuit configuration.

The crystal frequency cannot be calculated using the simple RC or LC formulas alone; its equivalent model and specified resonant frequency are needed.

Working Principle and Waveforms

At startup, a small disturbance is amplified and fed back. If the feedback has the correct phase and sufficient loop gain, oscillations build up. Nonlinearities or an amplitude-control network eventually limit the amplitude.

RC Phase Shift Oscillator

  1. The DC supply biases the transistor in its active region.
  2. A small signal is amplified by the transistor.
  3. The three RC sections shift the phase of the feedback signal.
  4. The feedback returns a suitable signal to sustain oscillation.
  5. The oscilloscope displays the output waveform.

Wien Bridge Oscillator

  1. The amplifier provides signal gain.
  2. The lead-lag RC network selects the oscillation frequency.
  3. At the selected frequency, the network has approximately zero phase shift.
  4. The amplifier gain and stabilization determine whether oscillation is sustained with a stable amplitude.

Hartley Oscillator

  1. The active device amplifies the signal.
  2. The inductor network and capacitor form the resonant circuit.
  3. The inductive divider feeds part of the signal back.
  4. The resonant frequency depends on effective inductance and capacitance.

Colpitts Oscillator

  1. The transistor provides amplification.
  2. The inductor and two capacitors form the resonant network.
  3. The capacitive divider returns feedback to the active device.
  4. The LC network selects the oscillation frequency.

Crystal Oscillator

A crystal mechanically resonates due to the piezoelectric effect. Its equivalent electrical network has a sharp resonance, helping stabilize the oscillator frequency.

Useful Formulas

RC Phase Shift: f ≈ 1 / (2πRC√6)
Wien Bridge (equal R and C): f = 1 / (2πRC)
LC Resonance: f = 1 / (2π√(LC))
Colpitts: Ceq = C1C2 / (C1 + C2)
Time period: T = 1/f

These are idealized formulas. Actual frequency can differ because of transistor capacitances, loading, component tolerances and the exact circuit topology.

Oscillator Examples & Problem Solving

Learn by solving numerical problems, circuit-design questions, derivations, waveform calculations and practical troubleshooting.

Topics: RC Phase Shift · Wien Bridge · Hartley · Colpitts · Crystal ·

Part A — RC Phase Shift Oscillator

Problem 1: Calculate the oscillation frequency

Given: R = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF. Assume the standard three-section RC network with equal components.

Formula:

f = 1 / (2πRC√6)

Substitution:

R = 10 × 10³ Ω
C = 10 × 10⁻⁹ F
f = 1 / (2π × 10⁴ × 10⁻⁸ × √6)
Answer: f ≈ 650 Hz
Problem 2: Calculate frequency for R = 4.7 kΩ

Given: R = 4.7 kΩ, C = 10 nF.

f = 1 / (2πRC√6)
= 1 / (2π × 4700 × 10 × 10⁻⁹ × √6)
Answer: f ≈ 1.38 kHz
Problem 3: Find the capacitance for 1 kHz

Given: f = 1 kHz, R = 10 kΩ.

f = 1 / (2πRC√6)
C = 1 / (2πRf√6)
C = 1 / (2π × 10,000 × 1000 × √6)
Answer: C ≈ 6.50 nF
Problem 4: Find R for 500 Hz

Given: f = 500 Hz, C = 10 nF.

R = 1 / (2πfC√6)
= 1 / (2π × 500 × 10 × 10⁻⁹ × √6)
Answer: R ≈ 13.0 kΩ
Problem 5: Determine the time period

Given: f = 650 Hz.

T = 1/f
T = 1/650 seconds
Answer: T ≈ 1.538 ms
Problem 6: Explain the phase shift

Question: Explain how the RC network and amplifier provide the required phase shift.

Answer: In the standard three-section RC phase-shift oscillator, the RC network contributes approximately 180° of phase shift at the oscillation frequency. The inverting amplifier contributes another 180°. The total loop phase shift is therefore approximately 360°, allowing positive feedback.

Problem 7: Calculate amplifier gain requirement

Question: What is the commonly quoted minimum amplifier gain magnitude for a standard three-section RC phase-shift oscillator?

Answer: Approximately 29.

This is an idealized result for the standard equal-section network. Practical gain requirements depend on the actual circuit and loading.

Part B — Wien Bridge Oscillator

Problem 8: Calculate frequency

Given: R = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF, with equal frequency-setting resistors and capacitors.

f = 1 / (2πRC)
= 1 / (2π × 10,000 × 10 × 10⁻⁹)
Answer: f ≈ 1.59 kHz
Problem 9: Design for 2 kHz

Given: f = 2 kHz, C = 10 nF.

R = 1 / (2πfC)
= 1 / (2π × 2000 × 10 × 10⁻⁹)
Answer: R ≈ 7.96 kΩ
Problem 10: Find frequency when R doubles

Question: If both equal frequency-setting resistors are doubled while capacitance remains constant, what happens to the ideal oscillation frequency?

f = 1 / (2πRC)
If R₂ = 2R₁, then f₂ = f₁/2.
Answer: Frequency becomes half.
Problem 11: Find the ideal amplifier gain

Question: At the balance frequency of an ideal equal-component Wien Bridge network, the feedback attenuation is 1/3. Find the ideal amplifier gain for sustained oscillation.

Aβ = 1
A × (1/3) = 1
Answer: A = 3

Practical circuits require an amplitude-control method to maintain stable oscillations.

Problem 12: Calculate the time period

Given: f = 1.59 kHz.

T = 1/f = 1/1590 seconds
Answer: T ≈ 0.629 ms

Part C — Hartley Oscillator

Problem 13: Calculate resonant frequency

Given: L = 220 µH, C = 1 nF. Assume L is the equivalent inductance.

f = 1 / (2π√LC)
= 1 / (2π√(220 × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁻⁹))
Answer: f ≈ 339 kHz
Problem 14: Find equivalent inductance

Given: L₁ = 100 µH and L₂ = 150 µH. Assume two uncoupled inductors connected in series.

L = L₁ + L₂
L = 100 + 150
Answer: L = 250 µH
Problem 15: Find the frequency using equivalent inductance

Given: L = 250 µH, C = 1 nF.

f = 1 / (2π√LC)
= 1 / (2π√(250 × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁻⁹))
Answer: f ≈ 318.3 kHz

In a real Hartley circuit, mutual coupling and winding polarity may change the equivalent inductance.

Problem 16: Explain Hartley feedback

Question: Why is a tapped inductor or inductive divider used in a Hartley oscillator?

Answer: It provides a fraction of the resonant-circuit signal as feedback. The active device supplies gain to compensate for circuit losses.

Part D — Colpitts Oscillator

Problem 17: Calculate equivalent capacitance

Given: C₁ = 10 nF, C₂ = 10 nF.

Cₑq = C₁C₂ / (C₁ + C₂)
= (10 × 10) / (10 + 10) nF
Answer: Cₑq = 5 nF
Problem 18: Calculate Colpitts frequency

Given: L = 100 µH, C₁ = 10 nF, C₂ = 10 nF.

Cₑq = 5 nF
f = 1 / (2π√LCₑq)
= 1 / (2π√(100 × 10⁻⁶ × 5 × 10⁻⁹))
Answer: f ≈ 225.1 kHz
Problem 19: Unequal capacitors

Given: C₁ = 6 nF, C₂ = 3 nF and L = 100 µH.

Cₑq = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) nF = 2 nF
f = 1 / (2π√(100 × 10⁻⁶ × 2 × 10⁻⁹))
Answer: f ≈ 356.8 kHz
Problem 20: Explain the Colpitts feedback network

Question: Why are two capacitors used in a Colpitts oscillator?

Answer: They form a capacitive voltage divider that provides feedback. Their series-equivalent capacitance, together with the inductor, determines the ideal resonant frequency.

Part E — Crystal Oscillator

Problem 21: Frequency and time period

Given: A crystal oscillator is specified to operate at 16 MHz. Find the period.

T = 1/f
= 1/(16 × 10⁶)
Answer: T = 62.5 ns
Problem 22: Compare frequency stability

Question: Why is a crystal oscillator generally preferred over a simple RC oscillator for a stable digital clock?

Answer: A quartz crystal has a sharp mechanical resonance, giving a stable reference frequency. An RC oscillator is generally more sensitive to component tolerances and environmental changes.

Problem 23: Crystal oscillator applications

Question: Explain three applications of crystal oscillators.

Answer: Digital clocks, microcontroller timing references and communication equipment that requires a stable frequency reference.

Part F — Oscilloscope & Multisim Problems

Problem 24: Find frequency from divisions

Given: Timebase = 0.5 ms/div. One cycle occupies 4 divisions.

T = 4 × 0.5 ms = 2 ms
f = 1/T = 1/0.002
Answer: f = 500 Hz
Problem 25: Calculate peak-to-peak voltage

Given: Vertical scale = 2 V/div. Waveform height = 4 divisions.

Vpp = vertical divisions × volts/div
= 4 × 2
Answer: Vpp = 8 V
Problem 26: Calculate percentage error

Given: Theoretical frequency = 10 kHz, measured frequency = 9.4 kHz.

Error (%) = |Theoretical − Measured| / Theoretical × 100
= |10 − 9.4| / 10 × 100
Answer: 6%
Problem 27: Troubleshoot a missing waveform

Question: A Multisim oscillator has a DC supply but no output waveform. List six checks.

  1. Verify transistor pin connections and bias.
  2. Check ground and supply connections.
  3. Check feedback-network wiring.
  4. Confirm component values and units.
  5. Check loop phase and startup gain.
  6. Verify the oscilloscope probe, timebase and transient simulation settings.
Problem 28: Explain the Barkhausen criterion

Question: State the criterion and distinguish startup from steady-state oscillation.

Answer: Ideal sustained oscillation requires loop-gain magnitude |Aβ| = 1 and total loop phase 0° or 360°. Startup usually requires loop gain slightly above unity; circuit nonlinearities or amplitude control limit the final amplitude.

Part G — Comparison & Circuit Analysis

Problem 29: Compare RC Phase Shift and Wien Bridge

Answer: Both use RC networks. A standard RC phase-shift oscillator uses three RC sections and an inverting amplifier; a Wien Bridge uses a lead-lag network and is commonly used for low-distortion sine-wave generation.

Problem 30: Compare Hartley and Colpitts

Answer: Hartley obtains feedback through an inductive divider. Colpitts obtains feedback through a capacitive divider. Both use an LC resonant network, but the feedback components differ.

Problem 31: Explain why oscillation frequency changes

Question: A resonant LC oscillator has unchanged inductance but increased capacitance. Explain the frequency change.

f = 1 / (2π√LC)
If C increases, √LC increases.
Therefore, frequency decreases.
Problem 32: Explain feedback and energy losses

Question: Why is an active device required in a practical oscillator?

Answer: Energy is lost in resistive elements and the resonant network. The active device supplies energy from the DC power source to compensate for these losses and sustain oscillations.

Quick Revision

Remember the Types

  • RC Phase Shift: three RC sections in the standard circuit.
  • Wien Bridge: lead-lag RC network; low-distortion sine wave.
  • Hartley: feedback from an inductive divider.
  • Colpitts: feedback from a capacitive divider; two capacitors.
  • Crystal: stable frequency reference using crystal resonance.

Formula Memory Tricks

RC: f is inversely proportional to RC.
LC: f is inversely proportional to √(LC).
Measured waveform: frequency = 1 / time period.

Common Viva Traps

  • Hartley and Colpitts are both LC oscillators, but their feedback dividers differ.
  • The ideal frequency formula is an estimate; the practical result may differ.
  • A waveform displayed by the browser demo is not proof that a Multisim circuit works.
  • For a crystal oscillator, use the specified crystal frequency and circuit design.

Before Your Lab Assessment

  • Explain the feedback path.
  • Calculate theoretical frequency.
  • Measure the simulated period.
  • Compare theoretical and measured frequency.
